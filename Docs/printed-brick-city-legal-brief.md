# Printed Brick City — Legal & Political Risk Brief

*This is a research summary, not legal advice. Laws differ by country and change often. Because the game distributes printable files, get a review from an IP lawyer and a lawyer familiar with firearm and imitation-firearm law before launch.*

---

## Risk Summary

| Area | Risk | Main mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Printable gun files | **High attention**, moderate legal risk | Solid toy props, non-standard connectors, careful wording, legal review |
| Minifigure-like characters | High if copied | Own proportions, hands, heads, joints |
| Newer/sculpted brick parts | Medium | Registry checks, human review |
| Basic bricks and dimensions | Low | No "compatible" claims, no branding |
| Real buildings / collapse imagery | Low–medium (PR) | Fictional city |
| Children's privacy (if online) | Medium | Plan compliance early |
| Player exports / sharing hub | Medium | Moderation, policy, takedown process |
| Printed-part safety | Low–medium | License + disclaimers |
| AI-generated assets | Low–medium | Human-authored designs, store disclosure |

## 1. Brick IP

### Generally safe
- **Basic geometric pieces:** bricks, plates, tiles, 1x1 round studs, slopes, round bricks, arches, basic hinges.
- **Why:** the original brick patents expired decades ago. Canada's Supreme Court (Kirkbi v. Ritvik / Mega Bloks, 2005) and the EU Court of Justice (Lego Juris, 2010) refused to protect the basic brick shape because the shape is functional.
- **Matching dimensions** (8mm pitch, 9.6mm brick, 3.2mm plate) is what clone brands do.

### Avoid or check first
- **Minifigure and its parts.** The figure's shape is a registered 3D trademark upheld in the EU. Avoid C-shaped hands, cylindrical stud-topped heads, minifig torso/hip/leg silhouettes, hair pieces, and printed-face styles.
- **Newer specialized elements.** EU registered designs last up to 25 years; US design patents 15 years. A 2021 EU General Court ruling confirmed interlocking toy pieces can still receive design protection. Check the EUIPO design database and USPTO design patents (owner: LEGO) for anything distinctive from roughly the last 25 years.
- **Sculpted parts** (animals, character heads, molded vehicle bodies, licensed-theme parts) may carry copyright, which lasts far longer than patents.
- **Official set builds.** Recreating a specific set's model copies a creative design. LEGO has won major cases against Chinese clone makers (e.g. Lepin) over this.
- **Branding:** the LEGO name, logo on studs, box/trade dress, official color names and part names/numbers.

### Marketing rules
- Never use "LEGO" in the title, store page, trailers, or tags.
- Don't claim compatibility. Neutral phrasing like "brick-built" or "printable bricks" is safer.
- Avoid packaging-style visuals resembling LEGO's.

### Your own brand
- Run a trademark search on your game title and any in-game brand/manufacturer names.

### Studying other companies
- Useful: Mega Bloks rulings, Lepin cases, and games like Brickadia and Brick Rigs.
- Caveat: a product existing doesn't prove it's legal — there may be licenses, settlements, or no lawsuit yet.

## 2. Guns and Printable Files

### Design rules (apply to every part, not just assembled guns)
- Solid interior; no open bore; no magazine well; no mechanical trigger assembly.
- Chunky toy proportions, toy color palettes, a distinct colored tip region.
- Central part named **"core" or "body," never "receiver" or "lower."** In US law the receiver is legally treated as the firearm itself.
- Connectors are **non-standard**: no Picatinny, M-LOK, or real firearm part dimensions, so prints can't mount or substitute for real accessories.
- Procedural generator and test mode enforce these rules before any export.

### Laws to review
- **Digital firearm file laws.** Several US states and some other countries (e.g. certain Australian states) restrict distributing or possessing digital files for manufacturing firearms. Non-functional props should fall outside these, but confirm how each law defines "firearm."
- **Imitation / replica firearm laws.** The US regulates toy and look-alike firearms (including blaze-orange marking for products in commerce); the UK restricts realistic imitation firearms; some jurisdictions regulate imitation firearms themselves. Non-realistic colors and shapes help.
- **Hosting policies.** Major STL sites and some platforms restrict weapon files, and moderators may not distinguish toy props from real designs. Check before relying on third-party hosting.
- **Physical sales.** If you ever sell printed kits, toy-safety and toy-gun marking rules apply directly.

---

## 3. Player Creations and Exports

- Build mode and gun test mode let players create and export their own designs. Someone will try to make something realistic or infringing.
- Snapped bricks make functional weapons practically impossible, but **appearance** can still be a problem.
- **If you add a sharing hub:** content policy, reporting tools, moderation, and a DMCA-style takedown process.
- Terms of service should cover who owns player creations and what's prohibited.

---

## 4. File Licensing and Safety

- **License the STLs**, e.g. CC BY-NC to allow printing but prevent commercial resale. Bundle the license with every export.
- **Disclaimers:** small parts / choking hazard, not safety-certified as a toy, print and use at own risk, keep from young children.
- Avoid claims that printed parts are safe toys.

---

## 5. AI-Generated Assets

- **Copyright ownership:** in the US, content without meaningful human authorship may not be protected, weakening your rights over purely AI-generated models. Keep humans authoring the designs, parameters, and final approval.
- **Contamination risk:** AI may reproduce existing LEGO shapes from training data. Design from functional descriptions, never part numbers, and review every part.
- **Store disclosure:** Steam requires disclosure of AI-generated content; check other stores' rules.

---

## 6. Ratings and Children

- **Toy look + guns attracts kids regardless of rating.** Expect ESRB/PEGI/IARC scrutiny of the combination; stylized brick violence helps.
- **If online** (accounts, chat, sharing hub): COPPA (US), GDPR child-consent rules (EU), UK Age Appropriate Design Code. A toy aesthetic can make regulators treat the game as child-directed.
- **Loot:** free found loot is fine. Paid randomized loot is restricted or regulated in some countries — avoid if possible.

---

## 7. Political and PR Risks

- **"3D-printed guns" is a headline phrase.** It's tied to real-world ghost-gun debates and violence. Avoid it in store pages, trailers, and press. Prefer "printable toy blasters" or "printable gear."
- **Timing:** a violent news event involving printed guns could bring sudden scrutiny. Have a short prepared statement explaining the props are solid, non-functional toys.
- **Skyscraper collapse imagery** invites 9/11 and current-conflict comparisons. Use a fictional city, avoid recognizable real towers, and be thoughtful with marketing shots of collapsing occupied buildings.
- **Real buildings:** some landmark appearances are trademarked; fictional architecture avoids this.
- **Brick-fan community:** may see the game as a knockoff. A clearly distinct printed style helps win them over.
